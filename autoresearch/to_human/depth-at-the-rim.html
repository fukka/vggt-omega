Depth at the Rim

Depth models are bad at the edge of a fisheye image. On a head-worn camera, the edge is where your hands are. Here is why it happens, and how far it can be fixed without depth labels.

DA3-Small · DA3-Large · VGGT · VGGT-Omega Aria Digital Twin · 2 rooms · 2 devices Every bar written down before the run
Part 1

Motivation

A fisheye lens packs more of the world into each ring of pixels as you move outward. Objects near the edge get fewer pixels. They also come out radially squashed.

Depth models are trained on ordinary photos. They have never seen this.

And the edge is where a wearable needs to work. The centre of the frame is the far wall. Your hands, your body, the desk — all at the edge.

How much worse is the edge?

We split each image by incidence angle — how far a pixel sits from the lens axis — and by true distance. Two zones matter:

  • Rim — beyond 38°, closer than 2 m. Hands, body, desk.
  • Centre — within 11°. Mostly the far wall.

Error is relative: how far off the depth is, as a fraction of the truth. Each frame is first aligned to ground truth by scale and offset, so global scale earns nothing. Only the shape counts.

Same 20 frames, four backbones. The raw numbers are not directly comparable (VGGT-Omega runs at a different patch size). The ratio is.

Part 2

Data

Everything runs on Aria Digital Twin — Meta camera-glasses recordings with dense depth on every frame. Two things about it matter.

Two rooms, not one. Six sequences in an apartment, two in a small office. The office used a different pair of glasses, with a different lens. That second room is our only honest test, and most conclusions below turn on it.

One apartment sequence is the same room with the furniture moved. A method can score well on the other held-out sequence just by memorising the room. This one catches that.

One detail that cost us a whole result

Room 2's lens differs by half a percent in focal length and nearly two degrees in field of view. Score it with the wrong lens and you get a smooth error growing toward the edge — which looks exactly like "bad at the rim". Every sequence is scored with its own calibration.

Part 3

Method

Take a strong depth model. Freeze it. Attach a small adapter — about 123,000 trainable parameters against a backbone of hundreds of millions. Nothing here retrains a foundation model.

Three rules make the results mean something.

  1. Every claim gets a twin. Each idea runs against a control identical but for the one thing tested. If the control improves too, the idea was not the cause. Five earlier methods here died to their own controls.
  2. The bar is set before the run. Success and refutation both go into git before anything executes. Several results below are failures against our own bars.
  3. The real test is a different room. Held-out sequences from the same room flatter everything.
A trap worth knowing

Because every frame is scale-and-offset aligned before scoring, a method that only rescales depth globally scores zero by construction. That is deliberate. Only a change in the shape of the depth map can win.

Part 4

Results

Seven findings, in the order we found them. Each one made the next possible.

4.1  Tilting the camera hurts — for the wrong reason

A head-worn camera rolls with the head. So how much tilt matters?

We rotated the virtual camera and re-rendered. We did not rotate the picture — rotating a picture adds black corners, and that turns out to be a separate problem entirely.

Extra whole-image error against each model's own level baseline.
BackbonePretrained on±20°±30°±40°
DA3-Smallsingle photos+13%+46%+131%
DA3-Largesingle photos+10%+52%+114%
VGGTmulti-view geometry+7%+11%+23%
VGGT-Omegamulti-view geometry+3%+11%+11%

Tilt sensitivity comes from the pretraining data, not from depth estimation.

Multi-view-pretrained models see cameras at every orientation by construction. They are 4–5× less sensitive at 30° and more accurate. Size does not help: DA3-Large is twice as accurate as DA3-Small when level, and slightly worse when tilted.

Then we measured real head tilt. The glasses record their own orientation and gravity, so this is read off the data. Across 60,105 frames: median 3.7°, 99th percentile 21.8°, nothing past 40°.

The model is flat to about ±20°. The data almost never leaves ±20°. Measured independently, they line up: 98.5% of real frames sit in the model's comfortable range. So gravity correction is not the win it looked like — at least indoors, on foot.

4.2  A hard black border is worse than tilting

This was an accident. Rotating a fisheye picture adds black wedges. We wanted to know what the wedges alone cost.

The most transferable warning here

DA3-Large is the best of the four on a clean frame and the worst with a border — a 7.4× collapse from a change that touches no scored pixel. Pick on clean-benchmark accuracy and you would choose it, then lose an order of magnitude to a letterbox, a crop pad, a lens hood, or a mask.

4.3  Teaching the model with no labels

The same frozen model is far more accurate when shown the scene as an ordinary photo instead of a fisheye. It knows what is out there. It just cannot read it through the fisheye.

So: take its answer from the rectified view, map it back into fisheye coordinates, and train the adapter to copy it. No depth labels anywhere.

The first version failed. To cover the whole fisheye cone, the rectified view must be so wide that a fifth of it is black — and per 4.2, that wrecked the teacher. A narrower view kept the teacher accurate but reached only 70% of the rim. Accuracy and coverage were in conflict.

Finding 4.2 broke the conflict. VGGT-Omega tolerates a border, so it can run the wide view at full coverage and stay accurate.

Same checkpoints, second room, other glasses. Nothing retrained.

The control transfers nothing. In the original room it carried most of the gain. In a new room it is worth zero. It learned "imitate a stronger model on these pictures", and that does not travel.

Every label-free method beats real labels once the room changes. Labelled training fits one room and cannot take it anywhere.

So what is transferable? We swapped the adapter for something that can express one thing only: a correction curve depending on incidence angle. Sixteen numbers, fitted on the apartment, applied unchanged everywhere.

Sixteen numbers carry everything that crosses a room. The other 122,884 fit the room.

At home the adapter is three times better. In a new room, sixteen numbers match it or beat it. The transferable part is a recalibration of depth against viewing angle — a property of the lens.

Two footnotes. The curve is smooth enough for a quadratic, so in practice it is six numbers. And it changes with the backbone — a calibration procedure, not a table anyone publishes once.

A separate line of this project fits the same curve from camera motion instead — no teacher involved. Same shape, same size (−15.4% against −16.6% on one sequence). Two unrelated label-free signals, estimating the same object.

4.5  Motion in the footage beats having the right lens

Should you fit the curve on the camera it will run on? It is the same lens, so it ought to win. We tested it on room 2's own footage — teacher-supervised, no ground truth anywhere in the fit.

It lost, badly.

The evaluation frames are identical across these rows. The only thing that changes is which footage the curve was fitted on.
Fitted onDeviceFootage→ Room 2, seq A→ Room 2, seq B
Apartment, 240 frwrongwalking−26.0%−8.9%

The wrong lens with motion beats the right lens without it — by about 10×.

So the fit does not really need your camera. It needs frames where the camera moved.

We can say why, because it was measured. Refit on random 30-frame subsets and see how far the answer moves between draws: 0.060 for walking footage, 0.127 for near-static. Static frames are worth about half as much each.

One honest limit. Room 2 is our only second device and our only near-static footage, so "fitting on the target device is bad" and "this footage is static" cannot be fully separated here. We read it as staticness because that is the part we measured directly, and because the curve that failed was that lens's own.

4.6  Three things that did not work

Each failed against a bar we had written down first. Each rules something out.

  • Wider teacher coverage. We pushed rim coverage from 70% to 99% using stretched views. Coverage rose; the teacher's accuracy there fell 9.5%, and the student got worse. A student follows the teacher's accuracy, not its coverage. No label beats a bad label.
  • Telling the network about the lens. We fed in the lens's local distortion per pixel, then tested on two unseen lenses. It lost to randomly shuffled values, and to feeding nothing. But it beat the wrong lens's values 16 times out of 16. So the network does read the input — real geometry is simply no better than an arbitrary fixed pattern, while contradictory geometry hurts.
  • Depth from camera motion. Triangulating across three frames gives metric anchors at about 4% error, where the model is at 44%. It beat both controls on all six sequences. But the locked bar asked the correction to fix the distortion, not just the numbers, and it failed that on all six. A recalibration, not a solution.

4.7  More data from the same room buys nothing

The obvious objection is that our training sets are small. We tested it: computation held fixed, only the number of distinct frames changing.

The bottleneck is not how much footage you have. It is how many different places it comes from.

Part 5

Conclusion

What to do

  • Never put a hard black border next to what you care about. No crops, no masks, no zero padding. To rotate, rotate the camera and re-render. Cost is set by proximity, not area — and on the wrong backbone it is catastrophic.
  • Pick the backbone for robustness, not clean-benchmark accuracy. They do not correlate. Our most accurate model fell apart at the first framing artefact.
  • You can adapt to a fisheye with no depth labels. Distil a stronger frozen model through a rectified view. Expect 80–90% of what real labels buy, in the room you trained in.
  • Expect almost none of it to transfer. What crosses a room is a small angle-dependent recalibration — six numbers — and that beats labelled training in a new room. About 30 frames fit it, but they must have motion in them: footage from the right camera does not make up for a camera that stayed still.
  • Do not fix generalisation with more footage from the same room. 48× more bought nothing and made another region worse.

What we are not claiming

  • Most results are one seed. The label-free result has three. The rest on consistent direction (16 of 16, 6 of 6) and on bars set in advance — not on any single large difference.
  • The cross-room test is 120 frames over two near-static sequences, and it now carries several claims above. It is the weakest link, and we would rather say so.
  • The tilt data is people doing household tasks, standing and walking. It says nothing about lying down, working under a car, or cycling — exactly where the assumption breaks.
  • In the tilt experiment we could not fully separate "changed the projection" from "removed the black border". A fisheye circle does not fit its square frame, so any rotation disturbs the boundary. That stays open.

What we would do next

  • Make room 2 a real test set instead of 120 frames.
  • Fit on moving footage from a third device. The Apartment curve beat room 2's own footage on room 2. That hints the curve may be largely device-independent — a one-off fit on any well-moving footage, not a per-camera procedure. It would also separate "static" from "different device", which room 2 alone cannot.
  • Combine the two label-free signals — the rectified teacher and the motion-triangulated anchors. They estimate the same thing, and each needs something the other does not.
